    Underground Pipe Damage: Common Causes and Modern Repair Solutions

    By Luis Ortega · August 31, 2026 · 5 min read

    What Goes Wrong Under the Yard

    Your sewer lateral and water service run from the house to the street, usually somewhere 2 to 6 feet underground. Nobody thinks about them until a toilet backs up or the lawn turns soggy. A typical lateral is 4-inch pipe, and older homes often have clay tile or cast iron. Each fails in its own way.

    Common Causes

    Tree Roots

    Roots find sewer lines by following moisture from a cracked joint or a loose gasket. A single fine root gets inside, then spreads into a mat that snags paper and grease. Clay pipe is the worst for this. The joints every two or three feet give roots a way in, and a mature oak or maple can fill a 4-inch line in a few seasons.

    Shifting Soil

    Nearly every older home has a basement, so foundation drainage and sump systems matter for keeping the lower level dry.

    Soil that settles or swells moves the pipe with it. A rigid clay or cast iron line cannot flex, so it cracks or pulls apart at a joint. Sags are common too. A sag, which we call a belly, holds water and waste, and on camera it looks like a stretch that stays full after flushing.

    Old Pipe Material

    Cast iron drains, galvanized supply lines and old lead service connections are still common in multi-family houses and older single-family homes.

    Age wears every material down. Cast iron rusts from the inside, and the bottom of the pipe can corrode through while the top still looks solid. Orangeburg pipe, used through the early 1970s, is pressed tar paper that slowly flattens into an oval. Old galvanized water lines rust shut inside and drop the pressure everywhere.

    Signs of a Buried Line Problem

    Several drains slowing at once points to the main line. One classic sign is a toilet that gurgles as the washer empties. Outside, look for a patch of grass that stays greener than the rest, or soft ground over the path of the line, especially in dry weather. Sewer smell near the cleanout is another.

    On the water side, the meter tells the story. Turn off every fixture and watch the leak indicator, the small triangle or dial on the meter face. A spinning indicator means water is leaving somewhere between the meter and the house, and a buried service line is a likely spot. Low pressure throughout the house can mean the same.

    A sewer camera settles it. A camera goes in at the cleanout and records the line, and a locator marks the depth and location on the surface. We show you the footage as it plays. The footage decides the repair.

    Modern Repair Options

    Trench repair is still the right call for some jobs. A collapsed section or a line with a deep belly usually has to be dug up, since lining needs a pipe that still holds its shape. Spot repairs can be small. One cracked joint might only need a four-by-six hole and a day on site.

    Trenchless lining avoids most of the digging. A felt liner soaked in epoxy resin is pulled into the line, inflated, and cured into a pipe within the pipe. Most residential liners go in through the cleanout and one small access pit, and the job takes a day. Roots cannot get through it.

    Pipe bursting is the other trenchless method. A bursting head pulled through the old line breaks it apart and drags new HDPE pipe into place right behind it. It needs a pit at each end but leaves the yard between them alone. Our crews at BWM Plumbing use both methods across Massachusetts, depending on what the camera shows.

    When to Call

    Do not wait on a sewer line that has backed up twice. Each backup leaves waste in the line, and roots grow faster with that steady food supply. A typical camera inspection takes less than an hour, and we hand you the video along with the marked location of the damage. Reach BWM Plumbing at (857) 335-4894. It helps if you know where your cleanout is, because that saves time on the visit.
