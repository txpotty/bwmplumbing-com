    How to Protect Your Pipes Before the Next Massachusetts Freeze

    By Andrew Blake · October 6, 2026 · 5 min read

    How Much Freeze Risk You Actually Have

    Water expands about nine percent when it freezes. The ice plug itself is seldom where the pipe splits. Pressure builds between the ice and a closed faucet, and the pipe gives way in that trapped stretch, which is why burst lines often show up a few feet from the coldest spot and only start spraying once things thaw. A single cold night can do it.

    Frozen pipes are a real winter risk across Massachusetts, especially during stretches of single-digit nights and strong wind.

    Pipe in an unheated crawlspace, an exterior wall, an attic or a garage is at the most risk, and so is anything on a wall that takes the wind head on. Those spots can drop below freezing long before the thermostat notices. Start your prep there.

    Outdoor Plumbing Comes First

    Disconnect every garden hose before the first freeze. A connected hose holds water against the valve and can crack it from the inside. Frost-free sillcocks have a long stem that shuts the water off inside the heated wall, but they only work if the hose comes off so the barrel can drain. For an old-style spigot, close the inside stop valve and open the outdoor tap to drain it.

    Sprinkler systems need their own shutdown. Shut the main irrigation valve, then crack the test cocks on the backflow device so it drains. Lines buried below the frost line usually come through fine, but the backflow assembly sits above ground, and a cracked brass body on a pressure vacuum breaker is one of the most common spring repair calls we get. Where winters run long, a compressor blowout is worth paying for.

    Protecting Pipes Inside the House

    Foam pipe sleeves are cheap and work. Buy the size that matches the pipe, usually 1/2-inch or 3/4-inch. Slide them over every exposed run in the crawlspace and garage, tape the seams, and pay extra attention to elbows and tees, since a gap at a fitting leaves the most vulnerable part of the line bare. On runs that froze before, a listed self-regulating heat cable under the foam is worth the money.

    On the coldest nights, open the kitchen and bathroom cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls so house heat reaches the pipes. Leave a thin drip running on both handles of a faucet on an outside wall. Moving water is slow to freeze, and an open tap relieves the pressure that actually bursts the pipe. If you travel, set the heat no lower than 55 degrees. Keep the garage door shut.

    Crawlspace vents and gaps around pipe penetrations let cold air blow straight onto the plumbing. Close foundation vents for the winter if your home has them. Seal holes where pipes pass through rim joists or exterior walls with expanding foam or caulk, because a pencil-sized gap with wind behind it can freeze a pipe inside an otherwise warm wall. That one is easy to miss.

    Find your main shutoff before winter. Most homes have one where the supply line enters the house and another at the meter. Turn it once a year so it does not seize, and if it is an old gate valve that will not close all the way, have it swapped for a quarter-turn ball valve, which takes a plumber about an hour on most homes. A stuck shutoff turns a small burst into a soaked floor.

    Water heaters in a garage or crawlspace need attention too. Wrap the first few feet of the hot and cold lines coming off the tank. A heater that sits in an unheated space and loses power or gas during a cold snap can freeze in its own lines, so know how to shut off the cold supply and open a hot tap to relieve pressure if that happens. Leave the tank full unless the room will stay below freezing for days.

    When to Call a Professional

    No water from a faucet on a freezing morning means a line is frozen somewhere upstream. Open that faucet and leave it open. Warm the suspect section slowly with a hair dryer or a space heater kept well back, and never use an open flame, because a torch on a frozen copper line can start a fire inside the wall or split the pipe from steam. Shut the main right away if the frozen spot is buried in a wall or a ceiling starts to drip, then reach BWM Plumbing at (857) 335-4894. Even after it thaws, the line needs a pressure check for splits before the water is turned back on.

    Fall Inspections Worth Scheduling

    A fall walkthrough takes about an hour. The tech checks exposed runs, hose bibs, the water heater and the main shutoff. Spots that froze in past winters get a closer look, and that is often the moment to talk about rerouting a line out of an exterior wall or replacing an old spigot with a frost-free model long enough to reach heated space. A fall repair costs less than a midwinter emergency.
