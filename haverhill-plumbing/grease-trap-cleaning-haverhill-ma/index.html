    A grease trap works by slowing the water down long enough for fat to float and food to sink. Past a quarter full, that stops. Grease rides straight through the outlet and coats the sewer lateral, where it cools into a hard white crust that a snake just punches a hole through.

    Slow sinks come first. Then gurgling floor drains and a sour smell near the dish area. Gray water rising through a floor sink means the trap is far past 25 percent and grease is already moving downstream.

    Under-sink hydromechanical traps and in-ground gravity interceptors make up most of our work. Automatic grease removal units are on the list too. Those AGRUs have a heater and a skimmer wheel that pull grease into a container, and they need the container emptied and the wheel and screen cleaned on a schedule.

    FOG stands for fats, oils and grease. Warm, it pours like water, and cool, it sets like wax. In a sewer pipe it sticks to the walls in layers, catches food scraps and wipes, and eventually closes the pipe down to a pencil-sized opening.

    That is usually hydrogen sulfide from grease breaking down in an overdue trap. A worn lid gasket makes it worse. Pumping the trap, scraping the walls and replacing a flattened gasket fixes most cases, and if it comes back fast we check the vent and the floor drain traps nearby for dry seals.

    It goes to a permitted receiving facility. Some facilities process it for biofuel or digesters. What is left gets dewatered and disposed of under the facility's permit, and each load is logged from your trap to their gate.
